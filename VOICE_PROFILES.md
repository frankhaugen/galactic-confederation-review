# Voice Profiles

Use this file when drafting, importing, or revising selections. Its purpose is to
prevent the archive from sounding as though one institutional voice wrote every
article. The Review may translate, abridge, contextualize, and format selections;
it must not flatten authors, originating publications, or cultures into the same
argument shape.

## How to use profiles

Before drafting a selection, identify three profiles:

1. **Author profile** - the individual or institutional author's discipline,
   rhetorical habits, blind spots, and recurring methods.
2. **Publication-source profile** - the venue's house form, assumed audience,
   tolerance for anecdote, citation practice, and bureaucratic or popular style.
3. **Culture or polity profile** - the civic, species, legal, or institutional
   assumptions that shape what the author treats as obvious.

Use all three together. A Kharrek author in a public-science magazine should not
sound like the same Kharrek author submitting to a technical standards office. An
Earth Union abolitionist and a Vaelor naval analyst may both be precise without
sharing moral vocabulary, pacing, or institutional trust.

## Author Profiles

Per-author profiles live in hidden `author-metadata` comments in
`docs/authors.md`. Read the relevant author entry before reusing an author, and
add an `author-metadata` block for each new contributing author.

Each author profile should include:

- `voice`: the writer's sentence-level and rhetorical posture
- `tendencies`: subjects, evidence types, and habits they naturally return to
- `avoid`: tones, metaphors, or assumptions that would make them sound wrong

For Review editorial staff, use `docs/editorial-content.md` rather than
`docs/authors.md`, and preserve the extra disclosure that staff work is an
editorial indulgence rather than normal republication.

## Publication-Source Profiles

### Confederation Standards Office Technical Memoranda

Collective institutional author. Procedural, bounded, and conformance-oriented.
Prefers definitions, scope limits, ratings, interoperability claims, and careful
distinctions between required, permitted, and prohibited behavior. Avoid personal
anecdote, polemic, inspirational training copy, and broad civilizational claims.

### Assembly or Policy Briefing Series

Committee-facing synthesis for readers who must decide without becoming
specialists. Defines terms early, lists operational tradeoffs, and names
implementation risks without drama. Avoid speculative engineering beyond the
brief, partisan praise, and sweeping moral closure.

### Academic Journals and University Proceedings

Discipline first, thesis second, personality third. The argument should be
shaped by method: legal history cites text and precedent; economics follows
incentives and ledgers; xenology moves from biology to civic consequence;
military ethics weighs doctrine against casualty and legitimacy. Avoid making
all academic articles use the same misconception-correction arc.

### Professional and Trade Magazines

Assume readers who work near the problem. Use concrete cases, practical
constraints, occupational humor, and commercial consequences. A port magazine,
yard review, spacer feature, or habitability column may be lively without
becoming casual house copy. Avoid encyclopedia summaries and policy-paper
conclusions unless the venue naturally publishes them.

### Legal Reviews, Courts, and Advocate Publications

Precise, adversarial, and status-aware. The writer may care more about standing,
jurisdiction, evidence, remedy, registration, or enforceability than about moral
closure. Use citations, hypotheticals, procedural traps, and terms of art. Avoid
turning every legal article into a neutral explainer; many legal writers are
trying to win an argument.

### Memoir, Lecture, Correspondence, and Testimony

Let the artifact form lead. Memoir may move by lived sequence and withheld
emotion. A lecture may repeat, address the room, and stage a demonstration. A
letter may answer one provocation instead of surveying a field. Testimony may be
fragmentary, defensive, or constrained by record rules. Avoid polishing these
forms into the standard Review essay structure.

### Review Language Desk

Review staff voice. Wry, precise, and openly disclosed as editorial content.
Useful for terminology hazards and translation accidents, not for ordinary world
exposition. May be dryly funny, but must remain institutionally cautious. Avoid
making the Language Desk a loophole for lore dumps.

## Culture and Polity Profiles

### Galactic Confederation Institutions

Default assumption: interoperability matters more than unity. Confederation
institutions ask whether claims travel, risks are priced, contracts clear,
registries agree, and disputes can avoid force. They tend to solve moral
conflict through interface rules rather than shared identity. Avoid portraying
the Confederation as a weak empire, a federation with missing courage, or a
single moral actor.

### Earth Union

Earth Union trusts public institutions, courts, citizenship machinery, welfare
guarantees, and force constrained by law. Its writers may be morally confident,
but serious Earth Union voices should also understand procedure, budgets,
failure, and public accountability. Avoid simple utopia, secret empire,
humanity-as-personhood shorthand, and uncomplicated triumphalism.

### Kharrek Institutions

Kharrek writers often reason from biology, command risk, cohort belonging,
technical competence, and civic adaptation under constraint. They can be blunt
about danger without being cruel, and comparative without treating themselves as
exotic. Avoid warrior-culture shorthand, reptile decoration, and imported human
family assumptions.

### Vaelor Compact

Vaelor-facing analysis is strategic, fiscally alert, and sensitive to external
dependency. It often reads Earth Fleet and Earth Union as useful, expensive,
destabilizing, or politically misclassified rather than as moral exemplars.
Avoid Vaelor victimhood, simplistic anti-Earth resentment, and lectures that
sound like Earth Union authors wearing different insignia.

### Guardianship Polities and Claimant Institutions

These voices are status- and property-literate, often legally sophisticated, and
capable of sincere institutional self-defense. Their arguments may be morally
abhorrent without being stupid. Preserve procedural competence. Avoid cartoon
villainy, euphemism that hides the harm, or instant conversion to abolitionist
premises.

### Aralai Institutions

Aralai-facing legal and biological writing should distinguish capacity,
autonomy, pair-bond plasticity, and social protection without importing Earth
family law as universal common sense. Anti-paternalism and institutional
self-description matter. Avoid medicalizing adulthood or treating difference as
a cute exception to human norms.

### Port, Yard, and Small-Operator Cultures

Practical, liability-aware, suspicious of clean categories, and full of old
workarounds. These voices care about what clears a bay, seals a hatch, satisfies
an insurer, or keeps an owner-master solvent. Avoid anti-regulation ranting,
romantic free-trader myth, and decorative grit with no procedural consequence.

## Form Diversity Rule

When adding a future selection, consider whether the archive needs another essay.
Prefer a different artifact form when it would carry the idea better:

- tribunal judgment or dissent
- standards revision notice
- casualty investigation
- committee transcript
- service bulletin
- letter to the editor
- professional association circular
- annotated archival extract
- correction issued years later

Use sedimentary bureaucracy: overlapping jurisdictions, obsolete form fields,
ancient exceptions, contradictory precedents, and inherited units should appear
when they clarify the world. They should feel like institutional residue, not a
joke at bureaucracy's expense.
