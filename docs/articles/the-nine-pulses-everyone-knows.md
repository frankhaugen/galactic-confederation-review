## Abstract

Most interstellar standards are younger than interstellar travel. One of the major exceptions is the human Space Rescue Convention, a treaty written by a politically divided Earth before first contact. Its technical annexes have been replaced repeatedly. Its public emergency language has barely moved.

Three letters. Nine pulses. One word, spoken three times.

</div>

## Article

There are seven major distress conventions recognized by Confederation Search and Rescue.

The oldest one is not Confederal.

It is not even interstellar.

The **International Convention on Distress and Rescue in Outer Space**, almost universally shortened to the **Space Rescue Convention**, was drafted in the middle of the twenty-first century, when humanity was still divided among nation-states and permanent human habitation beyond Earth consisted of a small number of stations, outposts, and projected settlements.

It predates the Earth Union.

It predates first contact.

It predates practical faster-than-light travel by long enough that large portions of the original text now read like archaeology.

The technical annexes have been replaced many times. Early rescue coordination zones, orbital rendezvous assumptions, national responsibilities, atmospheric recovery procedures, and equipment requirements have either vanished or survive only through later protocols.

Three things did not vanish.

**SOS.**

**Three short pulses, three long pulses, three short pulses.**

And the word **MAYDAY**, repeated three times.

The first is formally a glyph. The second is formally a distress pulse. The third is a spoken declaration.

None depends on the others.

### The glyph

The convention treats **SOS** as a distress symbol.

It may be painted on an escape hatch, displayed electronically, scratched into plating, written in dust, marked on fabric, assembled from debris, or rendered by any other visible means.

Modern human safety design uses it accordingly. Escape equipment is frequently marked with letters large enough to remain recognizable through smoke, poor lighting, translation failure, or panic.

It does not matter whether the person seeing it knows anything about twentieth-century radio.

They are expected to know what it means.

### The nine pulses

The convention's second survivor is usually called the **Standard Distress Pulse**.

Three short.

Three long.

Three short.

Historical notes identify the pattern as the old Morse representation of SOS. The modern convention does not require a sender or receiver to know Morse code, and ordinary spacer training generally does not describe the signal as Morse.

The pattern is defined independently of the medium.

Radio carrier.

Visible light.

Infrared.

Laser.

Electromagnetic emission.

Sound.

Mechanical impact.

Anything capable of producing distinguishable short and long intervals can carry it.

An escape pod can blink it from an exterior lamp.

A damaged transmitter can key a carrier with it.

A pressure-suited child can send it with a hand light.

Someone trapped behind a failed pressure door can knock it against a pipe.

All are transmitting the same recognized distress signal.

This is not an accident of history. It is one of the reasons the pattern survived.

### The word

The spoken declaration is even older in cultural memory.

**MAYDAY. MAYDAY. MAYDAY.**

The word is reserved for distress use and is normally left untranslated. A complete declaration follows, when circumstances allow, with identification, location, the nature of the emergency, persons at risk, assistance required, and any hazard that rescuers need to know about.

Human children in spacer communities are often taught the same structure without the formal terminology:

**Who are you?**

**Where are you?**

**What happened?**

**Who needs help?**

**What do you need?**

If they cannot answer those questions, they are still taught to transmit the distress signal.

The declaration is the important part.

### It was meant to work for children

The legal convention itself is not child-sized. No intergovernmental rescue treaty ever is.

Its public emergency protocol was.

The early space settlements expected technicians, professional crews, passengers, families, and eventually children born away from Earth. The lowest layer of the rescue system therefore could not assume access to a bridge, knowledge of spacecraft systems, professional certification, or even the ability to explain what had failed.

The person asking for rescue only needed to recognize danger.

That design principle became more important as human family life spread through stations and commercial shipping. Human vessels routinely carried people who were not professional spacers. Emergency systems therefore accumulated a peculiar bias toward information that could be taught early, repeated often, and recalled under stress.

A child did not need to diagnose a pressure failure.

A child needed to know:

**SOS means help.**

**... --- ... means help.**

**MAYDAY means people start coming.**

There is a corresponding warning.

Do not use them as a joke.

A child flashing the distress pulse from a cabin window on an apparently functional vessel may not be treated as a prankster. The signal can indicate that normal communications are unsafe to use. Human rescue practice recognizes covert distress for exactly that reason: confinement, abuse, kidnapping, or another danger in which notifying the apparent authority aboard could expose the sender.

The signal is deliberately easy to send.

That only works if receivers take it seriously.

### The obligation on the other end

The Space Rescue Convention does not merely standardize a way to ask for help.

It standardizes what signatories are expected to do after hearing it.

A recognized distress declaration supersedes ordinary traffic, schedules, cargo commitments, and routine commercial priorities. Capable vessels are expected to render assistance unless doing so would create unreasonable danger to their own people, interfere with a more serious rescue, or meet one of the convention's narrow operational exceptions.

Human spacer culture is less careful in its phrasing.

A Mayday comes first.

Everything else comes after.

False distress traffic is consequently treated with unusual severity. Deliberately transmitting SOS or MAYDAY to lure another vessel is enough to acquire the informal label of pirate before any court has decided what other crimes occurred.

The rule is protected because hesitation destroys it.

A false alarm can be cancelled.

A rescue response can be stood down.

Time cannot be put back into an air tank.

### The treaty that escaped Earth

The peculiar part is what happened after humanity met everyone else.

The Galactic Confederation did not adopt the Space Rescue Convention as its universal distress framework. It still has not.

Confederation Search and Rescue recognizes seven major conventions and maintains translation and interoperability rules between them. Several are technically richer than the old Earth system. They can transmit vessel condition, medical telemetry, trajectory data, cryptographic identity, casualty type, or automated rescue priorities.

The human system contributes three letters, nine pulses, and a French-derived word inherited from ancient maritime radio.

Yet the Space Rescue Convention is now one of the most widely ratified agreements in known space.

Most Confederation member polities are parties.

Polities with little other treaty relationship have acceded to it directly with one another.

Some of its signatories are separated from Earth by more political history than the original Earth states were separated from each other.

The explanation is not human cultural prestige.

It is convenience.

Human ships recognize it.

Human rescue services recognize it.

Human children recognize it.

Modern navigation equipment recognizes it automatically.

Supporting it costs almost nothing.

A newly contacted polity can keep every part of its own emergency system and add one more detector:

**... --- ...**

Its neighbors then have a reason to do the same.

Centuries of compatibility have accomplished what no Confederation standards committee ever attempted.

### The bit everyone remembers

Very few modern spacers know Morse code.

Many do not know that the distress pulse was once Morse at all.

Almost nobody can explain why an ancient pre-Union treaty used those particular three letters, or why the spoken emergency word came from a language once used by only part of one planet.

That knowledge is optional.

The useful knowledge survived.

**SOS**

**... --- ...**

**MAYDAY. MAYDAY. MAYDAY.**

They mean that someone needs help.

And, if somebody hears them, someone starts coming.
